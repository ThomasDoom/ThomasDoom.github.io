Power system for a Mars cave rover

NASA L’SPACE, Electrical Engineer, Sep - Dec 2025

What I did

I was the electrical engineer on a student team designing a rover to explore a cave on Mars, with no nuclear power allowed. I sized a 10,000 Wh lithium-sulfur battery and a sun-tracking solar array so the rover survives a 76-hour cave visit, 48 hours of it without sunlight. I ran the power trade studies, built the power budget across 13 operating modes, and wrote the power requirements. Through the challenges, I was able to step up to leadership roles and keep the team on schedule and successfully present our Preliminary Design Review to stakeholders despite the setbacks and challenges!

How it connects to NASA

NASA is building a Moon Base near the lunar South Pole and developing fission surface power so crews have energy regardless of location, weather or available sunlight. My rover faced the same problem on a smaller scale: no sunlight underground and no nuclear option. VIPER was designed for similar ground, driving into permanently shadowed craters. Trade studies, power budgets and requirements like mine are listed duties for NASA aerospace engineers at Johnson Space Center.

YUBI, a local intelligence system

Personal project, Dec 2025 - present

What I did

YUBI is a system I’m building to extend what I can do. It's meant to be one central system that remembers, reasons and acts across my programs and devices. From the very first day, I've developed YUBI with the project lifecycle methods used by NASA. Before writing any code, I set 82 exit criteria across phased milestones and ran a failure modes and effects analysis. Each part has been developed through strict requirements and heavy testing and today YUBI can hold conversations, keep a long-term memory, run a continuous thinking loop, and restart its own failed processes.

How it connects to NASA

NASA’s Moon Base plans include autonomous robots working near the crew. On the space station, Astrobee robots already work autonomously so astronauts spend less time on routine duties. YUBI is my testbed for that kind of crew support: a system built around one person’s work that can act on its own. Building YUBI with NASA systems engineering habits makes requirements, testing, and failure analysis part of my daily practice.

Living and working off grid in Hawaii

Verto Education, Hawaii Field Semester, Fall 2021

What I did

In Fall 2021 I lived off grid in Kalapana, Hawaii, in screened cabins in the jungle an hour from the nearest city, learning from local Hawaiian families. As their guests I farmed in sacred valleys closed to outsiders, cleared the overgrown canal that waters one valley, and helped build an imu, an in-ground oven of lava rock and banana leaves, to cook a feast for a festival. I led hikes from the front or stayed back to carry anyone hurt or tired, organized and led a stargazing event for 100 students, and did community service clearing trails and restoring paths and gardens. I also looked after a young girl from one of the host families all semester and took thousands of photos for the group, which connected me to everyone.

How it connects to NASA

NASA lists teamwork and inclusion among its core values, and NCAS staff said teamwork and communication can come from any experience, not only a job. With NASA funding, the University of Hawaiʻi plans to resume analog missions at HI-SEAS on Mauna Loa, on the island where I lived; crews there have spent up to 12 months isolated to study the challenges of long space missions. Hawaii gave me that at a small scale: a group living off grid, far from help, growing food and relying on each other. Learning from the Hawaiian families, on their land and by their rules, taught me to listen first and work as part of someone else’s team.
