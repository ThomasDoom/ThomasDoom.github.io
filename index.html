Power system design for a Mars cave rover

NASA L’SPACE, Electrical Engineer, Sep - Dec 2025

What I did

I was the electrical engineer on a student team designing a rover to explore a cave on Mars, with no nuclear power allowed. I sized a 10,000 Wh lithium-sulfur battery and a sun-tracking solar array so the rover survives a 76-hour cave visit, 48 hours of it without sunlight. I ran the power trade studies, built the power budget across 13 operating modes, and wrote the power requirements. Through the challenges, I stepped up to leadership roles, kept the team on schedule and successfully presented our Preliminary Design Review to stakeholders.

How it connects to NASA

NASA’s Human Spaceflight Mission Directorate is building a Moon Base near the lunar South Pole and its Research and Technology Mission Directorate is developing fission surface power so crews have energy regardless of sunlight or temperature. My rover faced the same problem on a smaller scale: no sunlight underground and no nuclear option. NASA’s VIPER lunar rover was designed for similar ground, driving into permanently shadowed craters. Trade studies, power budgets and requirements like mine are core tasks for aerospace engineers.

YUBI, a local intelligence system

Personal project, Dec 2025 - present

What I did

YUBI is one central system I’m building to extend what I can do: it remembers, reasons and acts across my programs and devices. From the very first day, I’ve developed YUBI with the project lifecycle methods used by NASA. Before writing any code, I set 82 exit criteria across phased milestones and ran a failure modes and effects analysis. Each part has been developed through strict requirements and heavy testing, and today YUBI can hold conversations, keep a long-term memory, run a continuous thinking loop, and restart its own failed processes.

How it connects to NASA

Autonomous robots are one of the technologies NASA is advancing for the Moon Base. On the space station, Astrobee robots already work autonomously so astronauts spend less time on routine duties. YUBI is my testbed for that kind of crew support: a system built around one person’s work that can act on its own, the same goal behind the spacesuit displays college teams design for astronauts in NASA’s SUITS challenge. Building YUBI with NASA systems engineering habits makes requirements, testing, and failure analysis part of my daily practice.

Living and working off grid in Hawaii

Verto Education, Hawaii Field Semester, fall 2021

What I did

I lived off grid in Kalapana, Hawaii, in screened cabins in the jungle an hour from the nearest city, learning from local Hawaiian families. As their guests we farmed in sacred valleys closed to outsiders, cleared the overgrown canal that waters one valley, and helped build an imu, an in-ground oven of lava rock and banana leaves, to cook a feast for a festival. I led hikes from the front or stayed back to carry anyone hurt or tired, organized and led a stargazing event for 100 students, and did community service for local Hawaiian families. I took thousands of photos for the group, which connected me to everyone, and one host family took me and two friends in as hānai, the Hawaiian tradition of informally adopting someone into the family, while we looked after their kids.

How it connects to NASA

NASA lists teamwork and inclusion among its core values, and NCAS staff said teamwork and communication can come from any experience, not only a job. HI-SEAS, a Mars-simulation habitat on Mauna Loa on the island where I lived, has new NASA funding to resume its missions, and NASA’s own CHAPEA crews spend 378 days in a 3D-printed Mars habitat at Johnson. Hawaii was similar on a small scale: we lived off grid, far from help, and relied on each other. Learning from the Hawaiian families, on their land and by their rules, taught me to listen first and keep an open mind to someone else’s methods and culture.

Pathway

NASA Career Pathway Alignment

My target role is NASA Aerospace Engineer, an Aerospace Technology (AST) position in federal job series 0861, working on human spaceflight systems. Since its May 2026 realignment, NASA has four mission directorates, and my work fits two of them: Human Spaceflight, and Research and Technology. Aerospace is one of the three engineering fields NASA hires most, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 8% growth for aerospace engineers from 2025 to 2035, about 3,800 openings a year.

My path to NASA

  1. Now, fall 2026

    Community College, A.S. Electrical Engineering

    • Completed: Calculus I & II, Differential Equations, Linear Algebra, Calculus-Based Physics I & II, General Chemistry I, Computer Science I.
    • Ongoing: Statics, Calculus III, Fundamentals of Communication. Circuits and Dynamics in spring 2027.
    • GPA: 4.00.
    • Experience: NASA L’SPACE Mission Concept Academy, NCAS Student Challenge, Personal Projects.

  2. Summer 2027

    A NASA internship (OSTEM)

    • Eligibility: U.S. citizen, 18 or older, enrolled in college with a 3.0 cumulative GPA. My community college is on NASA’s list of Eligible Institutions of Higher Education, which sets eligibility for internships funded by NASA’s Minority University Research and Education Project (MUREP).
    • Application: transcripts from every school, every paid job, experiences such as NCAS, skills, languages, and a personal statement of up to 1,200 characters for each project.
    • Deadline: summer 2027 applications close March 1, 2027.
    • My plan: apply to every summer 2027 project I qualify for on NASA STEM Gateway before March 1st, as well as any other aerospace industry internships too.

  3. Fall 2027

    A bachelor’s degree that qualifies for NASA engineering jobs

    • Degree: a bachelor’s in engineering from a school with at least one ABET-accredited engineering program, or in physical science, math or computer science. Most aerospace engineering jobs need a four-year degree.
    • Majors: aerospace engineering and engineering physics directly qualify me for AST titles.
    • From NCAS: an NCAS alum now working as an engineer at Johnson studied engineering physics.
    • My plan: finish the A.S. in August 2027, then transfer to an ABET-accredited B.S. in aerospace engineering or engineering physics.

  4. During the B.S.

    NASA Pathways

    • What it is: NASA’s student employment program, which can lead straight to a full-time NASA job. It needs a 2.9 GPA and 480 work hours before graduation.
    • Applying: through USAJOBS, with a federal resume of at most two pages that gives the month and year of every job, and a transcript that shows GPA. Experience must be described in a narrative style, using the CAR (Challenge - Action - Result) format.
    • My plan: apply early in the B.S., aiming for space suits and life support at Johnson Space Center, which runs NASA’s program for the Artemis III spacesuit and lunar rover and tests suits in its Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory.

  5. After the B.S.

    Entry-level NASA aerospace engineer (AST, GS-7)

    • Qualifying: GS-7 is the entry grade in NASA’s engineering postings. Some routes are a year of related engineering work, such as collecting data, keeping records and preparing technical documentation, or a year of graduate study.
    • The work at GS-7: engineering analysis, design, test or evaluation on assigned systems or components, solving technical problems under supervision, and preparing technical documentation.
    • Alternate options: NASA Force hires early-career engineers for 1 to 2 year terms on work like VIPER rover operations. Outside NASA, the Air Force’s Palace Acquire program puts new aerospace and electrical engineers through a 3-year paid training plan that ends in a permanent job.

  6. Long term

    The astronaut path

    • Education and experience: a master’s in engineering, science, computer science or math, then at least three years of related professional experience.
    • Health: vision correctable to 20/20, with glasses and LASIK allowed (I have bad vision), and medical, fitness and swim tests.
    • Ideal skills: operational experience making real-time decisions under stress, often built outside work through flying, diving or wilderness rescue, and teamwork as both a leader and a follower.
    • My plan: after the B.S., work as an engineer and earn a master’s, then apply after three years of experience. The path is worth it for the skills it builds, even if I am never selected.
    • Along the way: a pilot’s license, scuba certification, a crew at the Mars Desert Research Station, NASA’s Space Apps Challenge, and a SUITS team after I transfer.
